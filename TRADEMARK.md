The separation between licence and mark is deliberate and standard: you have broad rights to the software, and
narrow rights to the mark. Copyright and trademark are different instruments and
the licence only speaks to the first.

## What you may do without asking

- Use, modify, distribute and sell the software under Apache 2.0, including
  commercially and in closed-source products.
- **Say your project uses Euthynos.** "Built with Euthynos", "compatible with
  Euthynos", "an MCP client for Euthynos" — factual, descriptive references are
  fine and welcome.
- Write about it, benchmark it, criticise it, publish the results. We publish our
  own failures; we are not going to object to yours.
- Fork it. That is what the licence is for.

## What requires permission

- **Naming your fork or derivative "Euthynos"**, or a name confusingly similar.
  Call it something else. This is the ordinary practice for permissively licensed
  projects and is why the mark exists.
- Using the logo as your project's or product's identity.
- Implying endorsement, affiliation, partnership or certification.
- Using the mark in a company name, product name, domain, or app-store listing.
- Selling merchandise bearing the mark.

## Why this exists

A permissive licence deliberately gives away the ability to fork and sell the
code. The trademark is what stops a fork from also taking the identity — so that
when a user installs something called Euthynos, they know whose release it is and
whose evidence standards it was held to.

For a tool whose product *is* trustworthy answers, that provenance matters more
than usual. Someone shipping a modified engine under our name could attach our
credibility to output we never verified.
